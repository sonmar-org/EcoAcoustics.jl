#+TITLE: Candidate Acoustic Bands for Exploratory Analysis
#+STARTUP: showall overview

* Purpose

This note defines the acoustic frequency bands to calculate from the SM3M
recordings and explains why each was selected.

These bands are *exploratory candidates*, not a predefined set of predictors to
place simultaneously into a statistical model. The goal is to compare their
temporal behavior, source sensitivity, redundancy, and relationship with dolphin
presence and feeding before selecting a smaller, defensible subset for GLM/GAMM
analysis.

* Beach-week scope

For beach week, bands are computed from the matched SM3M broadband recordings
(see the beach README for the detection ↔ broadband pairing):
- *Inshore:* T-1C (SM3M 681787)
- *Midshore:* A-5C (SM3M 681863), W-1C (SM3M 681763, archived as "New1")
- 2017–2018 deployments (Apr/Jun/Oct 2017; Jul–Dec 2018)

*Offshore is excluded for beach.* The T-3C Snap (1-on/9-off duty → ~125 h of audio
over a 52-day span, presence-only, no usable ICI export) is marginal and dropped;
the offshore zone is deferred to the full Ch.2 analysis. So the beach set covers
*inshore + midshore only*.

*Calibration — required before computing band levels.* Apply the per-era SM3M
effective sensitivity: *−153 dBV/µPa through Mar 2018* (12 dB gain) and *−165 from
Apr 2018* (0 dB). Band levels across deployments are only comparable once this is
applied per era — do not mix eras uncalibrated. (See CLAUDE.md /
notes/data_decisions.md, "SM3M Gain Issue".)

* Candidate bands

| Band       | Type                     | Primary rationale                                                                                                            |
|------------+--------------------------+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 63 Hz      | One-third-octave level   | Standard low-frequency shipping-noise indicator; expected to respond strongly to large commercial vessels and distant shipping. |
| 125 Hz     | One-third-octave level   | Standard low-frequency shipping-noise indicator; complementary to 63 Hz and often responsive to vessel machinery and propeller noise. |
| 250 Hz     | One-third-octave level   | Exploratory extension above the conventional 63/125 Hz indicators; may be more sensitive to smaller vessels, fishing vessels, engine harmonics, and machinery noise. |
| 100–200 Hz | Broad octave-width band  | Captures low-frequency vessel and physical noise while providing a broader ecological summary than a single TOL.             |
| 200–400 Hz | Broad octave-width band  | Transitional band containing vessel harmonics, machinery noise, weather-related energy, and possible biological contributions. |
| 400–800 Hz | Broad octave-width band  | Retained because preliminary analyses showed relatively strong seasonal and diel structure; may contain information not apparent in broadband SPL or lower vessel-noise bands. |
| 1–4 kHz    | Broad exploratory band   | Candidate indicator for smaller, faster vessels (fishing vessels), whose cavitation, engine, propeller, and hull noise may extend higher than large commercial shipping. |

⚠ *Self-contamination caveat for 1–4 kHz:* this band overlaps the dolphin whistle
band (~2–20 kHz), so it may carry dolphin vocalizations rather than purely vessel
noise. Treat it as an exploratory vessel indicator with that caveat; the ≤ ~800 Hz
bands are the safer vessel/wind predictors. (SM3M Nyquist is 24 kHz, so 1–4 kHz is
well within range.)

* Why use both TOLs and broader bands?

The one-third-octave levels and octave-width bands serve different purposes.

The 63, 125, and 250 Hz TOLs provide standardized and externally comparable
measures of vessel-related sound. The broader 100–200, 200–400, and 400–800 Hz
bands provide simpler summaries of soundscape energy that may be more useful for
ecological interpretation.

Some overlap is intentional. For example, the 125 Hz TOL lies within the 100–200 Hz
band. Comparing them will show whether the narrower standardized measure adds
information beyond the broader ecological band.

* Why include 1–4 kHz?

Large commercial vessels often dominate lower frequencies, while smaller and faster
vessels can produce relatively more energy at higher frequencies through faster
propeller rotation, cavitation, engine harmonics, exhaust, and hull vibration.

The 1–4 kHz band is therefore included as an exploratory indicator for smaller
vessels and fishing activity. It is broad enough to avoid depending on a single
shifting tonal feature, but still narrow enough to remain interpretable. (See the
self-contamination caveat above.)

If this band appears useful, it can later be divided into 1–2 kHz and 2–4 kHz to
determine where the signal is concentrated.

* Exploratory evaluation

Before selecting predictors for a GLM or GAMM, each band should be assessed using:

1. *Temporal plots* — hourly and 3-hourly time series, diel cycles, seasonal
   patterns, and responses to known vessel passages or weather events.
2. *Correlation and redundancy* — compare correlations among bands. Highly
   correlated bands should generally not enter the same model unless there is a
   clear hypothesis and the model structure can support them.
3. *Association with external vessel data* — compare candidate bands with AIS
   vessel presence, type, distance, and speed where available. Fishing-vessel
   indicators should be evaluated separately from large commercial traffic.
4. *Association with environmental conditions* — examine relationships with wind,
   rain, and wave height to identify bands dominated by weather.
5. *Association with dolphin response variables* — compare each band with dolphin
   presence and feeding metrics using plots and simple single-predictor models
   before constructing multivariable models.
6. *Stability across sites and deployments* — determine whether a band behaves
   consistently across T-1C, A-5C, and W-1C, or whether its usefulness is
   site-specific.
7. *Distribution and extreme events* — compare mean, median, upper percentiles, and
   maxima. Short vessel passages may affect high percentiles or maxima more strongly
   than hourly means.

* Model-selection principle

The final statistical model should use a small subset of bands chosen because they:
- represent distinct acoustic processes;
- are not excessively redundant;
- have a clear ecological or source-related interpretation;
- behave consistently enough to support inference;
- add explanatory value after accounting for weather, season, site, and other covariates.

The exploratory band set is intentionally broader than the eventual model set.
